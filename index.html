Neighbourhood CoLab CIC

At the end of every consultation, something is dispatched to the patient. Nobody designed it for the patient.

A printed sheet. An SMS. A letter written for the record. The clinical moment is over and the system sends what the system has always sent. The After Note is a different answer to that moment, built from first principles, shaped entirely around one question: what does this specific person need to understand and do before they leave the building?

The gap that already exists

Every GP system has a dispatch moment. The clinician approves the summary and something leaves the building. In most practices, what leaves is a byproduct of the record rather than a communication designed for the person holding it.

Patients leave consultations with incomplete recall of what was said, uncertainty about their medications, and no reliable decision point for when to seek further help. This is not a technology problem. It is a design problem. The infrastructure to solve it already exists in every practice.

For AVT suppliers

The ambient capture moment already generates a structured summary. The After Note is what that summary becomes for the patient, without adding clinical workload.

For GP practices

Safety netting that is specific rather than generic. A documented, defensible record of what the patient was told and when. Fewer follow-up calls about medication instructions.

For patients

The After Note is free to receive and free forever. The commercial model is one penny per After Note, paid by the AVT supplier to Neighbourhood CoLab CIC. Every penny goes back into the mission. The patient is not the product. The penny is.

Where the After Note sits in the workflow

The After Note does not create a new workflow step. It occupies a moment that already exists in every consultation.

Every GP system has a button. It sends something to the patient at the end of every consultation. Usually a letter. The After Note does what the letter never could.

This works with dictation. It works with ambient voice. It works with typed notes. The dispatch moment has existed in every GP system for decades. The After Note is what comes out of it.

The letter still goes. The After Note goes with it. One is the record. The other is the guide.

The design principles

Nothing for completeness. Everything for utility.

Every decision about what goes into an After Note is governed by one question: would this specific person, in this specific moment, actually use this? The bar is use, not possibility. That question is what makes an After Note different from everything that currently exists in this space. It is not a patient-facing version of the clinical note. It is a different document, built for a different purpose, from the same source material.

Nothing that isn't already there.

The After Note contains nothing the clinician did not put into the approved summary. It selects, clarifies, and structures. It does not infer, interpret, or add. The GP's approved note is the boundary. This is both a design principle and a regulatory one: the current letter sent to patients from a clinical note has never been classified as a medical device. The After Note does the same thing, with more care. Staying within that boundary keeps it honest and keeps it safe.

Nothing held that doesn't need to be.

CoLab holds no patient data. No servers. No data harvesting. The After Note is generated from the clinician's approved summary and goes directly to the patient. The boundary is the GP's approved note. What stays within that boundary cannot be misused. This is both a design choice and an information governance one: the simplest architecture is the safest one.

A worked example

A patient with a pre-existing respiratory condition, worsening breathing, travelling to the Caribbean on Sunday. They came in scared their holiday was going to be ruined.

Clinical note

History

Face to face follow-up for worsening breathing. Pre-existing condition prone to causing breathing issues. Plans to travel to the Caribbean on Sunday. Concerned about enjoying the holiday. Exercises regularly but currently finds any exercise tiring. No red flag symptoms.

Medications

Foster inhaler, carbocisteine.

Examination

Mild wheeze and a few crackles noted.

Diagnosis

Worsening breathing.

Plan

Antibiotics twice daily for seven days. Oral steroids once daily for five days. Take with food. Alongside existing medications.

Safety netting

If not better by Sunday, book Thursday or Friday. If symptoms worsen, return.

After Note

Your breathing and what to do before Sunday

You have been prescribed a short course of antibiotics and steroids to help improve your breathing over the next few days.

What to take

Antibiotics

Take twice a day for 7 days, with breakfast and your evening meal.

Steroids

Take once a day for 5 days, with breakfast. Take with food if possible.

Your usual inhalers and carbocisteine

Continue these as normal.

Before you travel: the aim of this treatment is to improve your breathing before your trip. If you are not improving by Sunday, contact the surgery before travelling.

Get medical help sooner if

  • your breathing gets worse
  • you feel short of breath at rest
  • you develop chest pain
  • you feel significantly more unwell, feverish, or confused
  • you are struggling to speak in full sentences

If symptoms are severe or sudden, seek urgent medical help.

Going abroad

What to do if you become unwell while travelling

From your GP at Weymouth & Portland PCN. Questions? Contact the surgery.

The decisions, explained

The title

The clinical note says "worsening breathing." That's a diagnosis. The After Note title says "your breathing and what to do before Sunday." That's the reason they came in. The patient didn't come in with a respiratory presentation. They came in worried about their trip. The title names what they're actually facing without telling them how to feel about it.

The medication cards

The note names antibiotics and steroids but doesn't tell the patient how to take them in a way they'll remember. The cards strip everything back to what matters: what, when, with what. Plain language throughout. The existing medications are grouped under "your usual" to signal that nothing changes with these. The focus stays on the two new things they need to remember.

The callout

The safety netting in the clinical note is written for the record. The callout in the After Note is written for the person. The framing centres the aim of treatment and gives a clear decision point. Crucially, it avoids implying that travel is clinically endorsed. The note reflects the patient's situation. It doesn't make commitments the consultation didn't make.

The safety netting

The original clinical note says "if symptoms worsen, return." That's correct but not specific enough for real-world decision making. Patients need to know when to wait, when to seek help, and when something is potentially serious. The After Note names the symptoms that warrant urgent action. This is both more useful to the patient and more defensible clinically.

The one link

We could have added NHS 111, the surgery website, a breathing exercises guide, a travel insurance reminder. We added one link. The foreign travel advice page answers the practical question the consultation couldn't fully address: what do I do if I get ill while I'm away? One link gets clicked. Eight links get ignored.

What was left out and why

These are the things from the clinical note that didn't make the After Note. Every omission was a decision.

Mild wheeze and crackles

Examination findings. Written for the clinical record. The patient can't act on this and it would worry them without purpose.

Diagnosis: worsening breathing

They already know their breathing is worse. Labelling it adds nothing. The After Note addresses the situation, not the classification.

Pre-existing condition detail

They've lived with this condition. They don't need it explained back to them at the end of a consultation.

Exercise currently tiring

They told the GP this. Reflecting it back is noise. The medication addresses it. The After Note focuses on the medication.

Who is behind this

Neighbourhood CoLab CIC was founded by four people who have been friends since childhood. Between them: a nurse, a clinical psychologist, a social worker, and an applied philosopher.
